Fly Lab · the maze guide

A fly-brain helicopter that finds its way

Can a tiny fly-like brain that has flown a route once or twice find its way back to it, from anywhere, using only a camera that points at the ground?

This is the second guide in the Fly Lab series. The first one teaches a simulated fly to tell faces apart. Here the same kind of brain leaves the lab and hovers over a hedge maze in the countryside, like the Queen's garden in Alice in Wonderland. It is shown a route through the maze. Then it is dropped somewhere else, facing any way, with no map and no line on the ground, and has to find the route and follow it to the exit. The scoreboard at the side keeps count of how often it makes it.

14 × 20 camera pixels10–20 thousand memory cellsa route = 2.5 KBno map, no GPSdropped anywhere: 90–100%
The short version Like an ant, the fly does not remember "turn left here". It remembers what the ground looked like along the route and steers toward whichever direction looks most familiar. That alone gets lost in the look-alike hedge corridors: a simple fly released near the route reached the goal 13% of the time. Keeping a running guess of where it is on the route lifted that to 70%. Learning flights and a memory of where every spot on the map looks like let it be dropped anywhere and still reach the goal (95–100% in the lab). A contrast filter keeps it working in fog and dusk. Everything in this guide is a simulation, and you can run a small version of it right here in your browser.

Chapter 1

The world it flies over

What does the fly have to find its way through, and why is it built the way it is?

The maze stands in the countryside. Its hedges (tall and low, some with roses, some old stone walls, a few topiary balls) bend a little and vary in thickness and height. They stand on a lawn mowed in stripes, inside a gravel path. Around it are fields of different kinds (meadow, wheat, ploughed soil, rows of crops, orchards) with hedgerows between them, roads with centre lines, houses and barns, groves of trees and sometimes a pond. Everything has a height and a shadow from a low sun. The whole world is made by code, so any maze number gives a new one.

The first version of this world was a patchwork of pink checkers and blue ponds. It was much easier: every spot had its own colourful fingerprint. Inside a real hedge maze every corridor looks nearly the same, and that turns out to be the hard part.

Chapter 2

What the helicopter sees

How much of the world does the fly's camera actually get?

The helicopter is ideal: it always knows its compass heading, its speed and its height, and its camera always points straight down. The camera is the same eye as in the first guide, 14 rows × 20 columns = 280 sensors, lying on the ground below and turning with the fly. At the normal height it covers about 1.6 × 2.3 maze cells. Each sensor reports brightness and two colour differences (red–green and blue–yellow), and that is all the brain ever gets: 840 numbers per look.

The ground under the camera

What the fly gets: 20 × 14 sensors

Lower down, the camera sees a smaller, sharper patch. Higher up it sees more, blurrier. Try to find a spot where you can tell, from the 20 × 14 picture alone, where you are. That is the fly's job.

The brain behind the camera

It is the same brain as in the first guide, cut down to what this job needs: a memory centre and nothing else. The memory centre has 20,000 Kenyon cells in the lab (10,000 in the small version on this page). Each cell listens to 6 random brightness sensors and 3 random colour signals, and only the 1% that are most excited fire. So every view of the ground becomes a sparse pattern of about 100–200 firing cells, a kind of fingerprint.

Chapter 3

Learning a route, the way an ant does

How can a fly follow a route without ever being told "turn left here"?

Ants and bees can retrace a route they walked a few times, and the leading idea is not that they memorise turns. They remember what the world looked like along the way, and at every step turn toward whichever direction looks most familiar. The fly's memory centre does exactly that:

  1. Fly the route a few times (with a teacher steering). Every view makes the Kenyon cells it uses "familiar". That is the whole memory: which cells have been used. Nothing about the route is stored as a path.
  2. To navigate, look in 9 directions (60° left to 60° right). For each, count how many of the firing cells are still unfamiliar. Fly the direction with the fewest.

Below is a small version of it that runs in your browser. Teach it the route, put it down near the route facing the wrong way, and watch. The bars show how unfamiliar each direction looks (short = familiar).

Chapter 4

Keeping track instead of guessing afresh

What if the fly did not decide from scratch at every step?

A single view is ambiguous in a look-alike corridor. A sequence of views, together with how far the fly has flown, is not. The ideal helicopter knows its heading and speed exactly (insects get the same from path integration), so it can keep a running guess of how far along the route it is:

  1. While learning the route, it also stores the pattern of firing cells at points along it, in order.
  2. While flying, it keeps a belief over "where on the route am I?". Each step the belief is moved on by its own flying, blurred a little for uncertainty, and sharpened by how well the current view matches the views stored there.
  3. It then prefers directions that match the route's direction at its best guess, as firmly as it is sure, and lets the familiarity scan correct sideways drift.

Go back to the widget in chapter 3, switch the steering to "+ keep track" and repeat "Try 8 releases near the route". In the lab this took a single fly from 13% to 70% (and from 47% to 91% of the route flown). A short gap where the route is out of sight (up to 1.5 cells) is bridged by its own motion; only a longer loss resets the belief.

Try this The in-page fly is smaller than the lab's (10,000 cells, a 352-pixel map, 8 releases) so its numbers differ, and 8 releases is a small sample: expect the same direction, not the same figures.
70%Where this leaves us
A fly that remembers where it has been follows the route through look-alike corridors. But we only ever released it next to the route. Next: drop it anywhere.
Chapter 5

Dropped anywhere

How can the fly find the route from a place it has never been near?

Ants do not only learn the route. Around their nest they make learning walks, looking back at the nest from many spots, which lets them find their way home from nearly anywhere close. The maze fly copies that:

  1. Learning flights. After learning the route, the fly flies from spots all over the map toward the route and keeps those views in a second memory (the mushroom body keeps different memories in different compartments). This one is graded: each view halves the "novelty" of the cells it uses. A plain yes-or-no memory fills up (94% of cells familiar) and stops telling directions apart.
  2. Two-stage navigation. Until the route itself looks familiar, follow the second memory; then switch to the route memory.
  3. Know where you are on the map. During the learning flights the fly also stores, for every spot, the view turned to face north (its compass lets it turn its camera image, so one stored view per spot fits whatever way it faces). Dropped somewhere, it keeps a belief over the whole map, moved by its own flying and sharpened by matching, and once sure flies straight for the route.

A single look is usually enough but not always: for this small in-page fly, across 40 random spots in clear weather, the best guess was within 0.6 of a maze cell 75% of the time (median error 0.3 cell), and a look-alike place fooled it the rest. In fog without the filter it was never right (0%); with the filter, 65%. The real flight does not rely on one look: the fly keeps a belief that its own motion carries forward and each new look sharpens, and in the lab its guess was then typically off by only 0.15 cell. Chapter 6

High and low

What happens when the helicopter is lower or higher than when it learned?

A helicopter cannot always fly at the same height. Height changes what the camera sees: lower, a smaller and sharper patch; higher, a larger and blurrier one. Use the height slider in the camera widget in chapter 2 to see it. For the fly it is the same problem as viewing distance was for faces, and the lab's numbers show it clearly: a fly that learned at one height and is flown low does worse than a fly flying blind. Every view looks unfamiliar and the answers all tip the same way.

  • Keep a memory per height. The helicopter knows its height, so it can use the memory made at the closest height. This helps most when low.
  • Use a small swarm (chapter 8): about +7 points on top.

Height is manageable if the fly has practised at several heights and knows its altitude. Weather is next.
Chapter 7

Fog, dusk and a noisy camera

What if the day is not the day it learned the route?

The same landscape looks different in different weather. Haze and fog fade everything toward grey, dusk is dark and orange, night is dark and grainy, and a cheap camera adds speckle. A fly that learned the route in clear weather sees a route full of unfamiliar views and gets lost. The cure is a contrast filter: stretch every view so its brightness always has the same spread, like the gain control in real eyes. Move along the route and try the weather:

Chapter 8

Many flies, one camera

How do the swarm tricks from the face guide carry over?

The first guide found that a swarm only helps if its members see differently. Here the swarm is a set of "virtual flies" sharing one camera frame and one wiring: each member looks at its own patch of ground (its own centre of gaze), and every member's opinion about the nine directions is added up. Each extra member costs only its own 20,000 familiar-or-not flags. A drone camera has far more pixels than a fly's eye, so a wide, sharp frame can be divided among members.

Chapter 10

How big a maze, and how long would it take?

Could this fly a real area, like 100 × 100 maze cells, or 1,000 × 1,000?

Training time is dominated by the learning flights, because they cover the whole map, so it grows with the area. Learning the route itself grows only with its length. Move the sliders to plan a real flight: one maze cell is taken to be about the flying height, since the camera sees a patch of ground roughly 1.5 times as long as it is high.

Bigger maps need four changes: build the landscape in tiles around the helicopter; search only near the believed position; keep a separate memory for each region (the route memory already fills half its cells by 6 × 6); and learn in a corridor around the route instead of the whole map. 1,000 × 1,000 is not possible as built.

The recipe scales to about a kilometre in principle, at hours of computing and a few hours of real flying with a survey. Beyond that, the memory needs splitting.
Chapter 11

Where else the same trick could work

Who would want a tiny brain that repeats a route it was shown once?

"Visual teach and repeat" is a real technique in robotics: show a robot a route once, and it repeats it using a camera. What is unusual here is how small it is: one yes-or-no flag per memory cell, 2.5 KB per route, no map, no GPS, and a brain that could run on a camera board costing a few dollars. Some ideas, none tried outside the simulation:

JobWhat is taughtWhat could go wrong
Drone inspection where GPS is weakA pilot flies the route once (a barn, a greenhouse, under a bridge, along a warehouse aisle); the drone repeats itChanging light, moved goods, wind

Farm rows and orchardsOne pass along the rows; repeat each weekCrops grow and change colour: the memory ages
Robot lawnmowers and delivery robotsWalk the route once with a remoteMoving people and cars; a camera looking forward, not down
Pipes, tunnels and underwaterA route through look-alike surroundings, where keeping track (chapter 4) matters mostVery little to see; murky water is "fog" (chapter 7)
Many cheap cameras votingThe swarm from chapter 8, one camera or severalEach camera must be trained where it stands

The same brain, other jobs

Telling things apart

The face guide's fly learns from yes/no feedback on a device with no network: sorting ripe from unripe fruit, flagging defects on a line with an operator's button as the reward, camera traps sorting "animal / no animal".

Many looks, one decision

Several cheap cameras looking at slightly different parts of one object and voting: a bigger gain than the same view repeated, as the face guide found.

Anything with a sparse memory

Any job that can be phrased as "have I seen something like this before?" fits the yes-or-no memory: place recognition, anomaly spotting, "is this the usual scene?"

Chapter 12

What this does not show

How far can the results be trusted?

  • It is a simulation. The world is generated by code, not photographed. The pictures are drawn, not real, and nothing here has flown a real drone.
  • An ideal helicopter. It knows its heading, speed and height exactly, the camera always points straight down, and there is no wind or drift. A real drone drifts, and its "known" numbers are estimates.
  • A still world. No cars or people move, the sun never moves, nothing grows. Weather is simulated as changes to the picture (fade, darken, tint, speckle), not photographed.
  • Small samples. Each figure comes from 20–40 drops, good to about ±8 points, on a few mazes of one kind.
  • Small mazes. The lab's results are mostly on 6 × 6 mazes, with a few on 10 × 10. Bigger mazes hit memory and compute limits (chapter 10).
  • The in-page fly is smaller. The widgets use 10,000 cells and a 352-pixel map so they run in a browser tab; their numbers differ from the lab's, and 8 releases is a tiny sample.
  • Hardware estimates are estimates. The camera-board sizes and times come from counting operations, not from a board.
95%Where this leaves us
Good enough to be worth a real test, far from proof. Try it yourself next.

Try it, run it

How do I fly it myself?

Open the Maze app Build a maze (size, wobble, variety), teach the fly the route at chosen heights, tap anywhere on the map to drop it, and watch what it sees and how unfamiliar each direction looks. Change its height mid-flight, switch on the swarm layouts, the wrong-way memory or climbing, pick the weather and the contrast filter, and fly over a changed version of the maze.

To reproduce the lab's numbers, the command-line tool tools/route.mjs in the flyfy repository runs the same experiments. For example, the full setup, dropped anywhere, in fog with the filter:

Chapter 15

Words to know

Familiarity navigation
Following a route by turning toward whichever direction looks most familiar, the way ants and bees are thought to do it.
Kenyon cells
The fly's learning centre. Each cell reads a few random inputs and only a few percent fire at once, so every view gets its own sparse code.
Route memory
One yes-or-no flag per Kenyon cell: has this cell been used while flying the route?
Approach memory
A second, graded memory made during the learning flights, used to find the route from anywhere.
Learning flights
Flights from many spots toward the route (or nest), used to learn what the way back looks like from everywhere.
Path integration (odometry)
Keeping track of where you are from your own movement: heading and distance flown.
Belief
A spread of guesses of where the fly is, each with a weight; it is moved by flying, blurred a little, and sharpened by what the camera sees.
North-up view
The camera image turned to face north with the compass, so views taken facing different ways can be compared.
Contrast filter
Stretching each view so its brightness always has the same spread, so fog and dim light stop changing what the fly sees.
Swarm
Several virtual flies sharing one camera and one wiring, each looking at its own patch; their opinions are added up.
Drop
Putting the fly down somewhere on the map, facing any direction, and letting it find its own way.
Maze cell
The unit of size here: one square of the maze, about the flying height when seen from a real drone.
