Fly Lab · a plain-language guide

Teaching a fly to tell faces apart

Can a fruit fly that learns only from small rewards and small pains tell a man from a woman in a photo? And how far can we push it?

That one question runs through this whole guide. We follow a single photo on its way through a simulated fly: into its eye, through its neurons, down to the foot it presses. Along the way we meet each part of a real fly it is modelled on, the limits we ran into, and everything we tried to raise the score. The scoreboard at the side keeps count.

280 light sensors400 memory cells3,330 photosguessing = 50%one fly ≈ 61–63%best swarm ≈ 67–68%
The short version One fly gets about 61% of never-seen faces right, and about 63% if it looks a few times. A small swarm of flies, each looking at a slightly different spot, gets about 66%, which is what the best simple "straight-line" reader can get out of these photos. A swarm whose new members practise on the photos the others find hard gets 67–68%, the first result past that line.
Chapter 1

A real fly's senses

What does a real fruit fly have to sense the world with, and which of those senses does our fly get?

A fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster) is about 3 mm long and senses far more than light. It smells with its antennae, tastes with its feet, hears with a joint in its antennae, feels the air on its bristles, and keeps its balance in flight with two tiny gyroscopes. Our simulated fly only needs a few of these for the photo test, but it helps to know what the real animal has.

Chapter 2

The fly's brain

Which groups of neurons turn light into a decision in a real fly?

A fruit-fly brain has about 140,000 neurons, and in 2024 its complete wiring diagram, the connectome, was published: every neuron and every connection. Well over half of those neurons sit in the two optic lobes, which shows how much of a fly's brain is devoted to seeing. From the eye, the signal passes through a chain of neuron groups, each doing one kind of job.

Chapter 4

How a fly sees

What is left of a photo once it has passed through a fly's eye?

A compound eye is a dome of tiny tubes (ommatidia), each pointing in a slightly different direction and reporting one blob of light. There is no sharp image anywhere, only a coarse mosaic. Our fly has 14 rows × 20 columns = 280 sensors spread over a 100° × 70° view; the photo fills about 70° × 50° of it.

Right behind the sensors, neighbours dim each other (the lamina's lateral inhibition), so edges pop. Further in, cells respond to the direction edges run in, to small dark or bright spots, and to things that grow fast. Some flies also have a fovea: sensors packed more tightly in the middle of the view.

Chapter 5

The test and its limits

How do we test the fly fairly, and what makes this test so hard?

A trial is simple. A photo appears. The fly has half a second to press the left foot (man) or the right foot (woman). A right answer earns a small reward, a wrong one a small pain, and that is all it ever learns from. After practice it takes an exam on 500 photos it has never seen, with men and women counted equally, so guessing scores exactly 50%.

Watch out The photos are not lined up. Faces sit in different places, at different sizes and angles, sometimes with other people in the frame, and a few are mislabelled. That is the hardest part of the task and it caps what any simple reader can do. (We also checked for copies: only 6 exam photos, 1.2%, have a near-copy among the practice photos.)

Three bottlenecks

Information gets lost at three points: when the photo is shrunk to 32 × 32, when 280 sensors sample it, and when the fly's simple learning rule reads what is left. A first fly with just a memory centre, on grey photos, scores 53.6%: barely above guessing.

Chapter 6

The 66% line

How well could a perfect student do on these photos, and what does "straight-line reader" mean?

Imagine every photo as a dot on a map, placed by its features. If men's dots and women's dots formed two separate islands, one straight line could split them perfectly. They don't: the clouds overlap a lot. A straight-line reader (in maths: a linear classifier) draws the best single dividing line it can. Wherever the clouds overlap, some dots end up on the wrong side, whatever you do.

We measured the best possible straight line on the photos themselves, describing each photo in several different ways. The best description we found (edge directions summed over small patches, plus colour) reaches 66%. That is the ceiling for any reader that works by adding up weighted evidence, and the fly's learning synapses are such a reader.

Under the hood "Best straight line" is shrinkage LDA: a linear classifier that corrects for inputs that move together. "The fly's own learning rule" is plain class averages on the raw signals, because reward-and-pain learning simply adds up "this pattern came with a man" and "this pattern came with a woman". That is why gain adaptation (each cell rescaling its own signal) matters so much in the last row.
Keep in mind 66% is not a law of nature. A deep neural network could very likely do better on the same photos. It is the ceiling for simple, fly-like readers on these 32 × 32 photos, and a swarm of flies that specialise in different photos is not one straight line, so it can get past it (chapter 8).
53.6%Where this leaves us
There is room between 53.6% and 66%, and the table already hints where it is: colour, and edge directions read with gain adaptation. Next: five rounds of trying to close that gap.

Chapter 8

A swarm of flies

Can many flies voting together beat one fly, and even the 66% line?

Many flies can vote. That only helps if they make different mistakes: ten flies that all see the same thing are wrong on the same photos. So each fly gets its own centre of gaze, a point on a small circle around the middle of the picture. With the sharp centre switched on, each fly sees a different part of the face in detail.

Boosting: newcomers learn from the swarm's mistakes

Flies join in rounds of five. After each round the swarm answers every practice photo, and the photos it gets wrong, or only just right, are shown more often to the next round (AdaBoost weights, capped at 4× so the swarm doesn't chase the few mislabelled photos). The newcomers become specialists: on their own they score only 49–54% on the exam, because they get ordinary photos wrong. Inside the swarm they cover its blind spots.

On a tiny computer

Could a real swarm run on cheap hardware?

One fly is small enough for a camera board costing a few dollars, such as the ESP32-CAM. These are estimates from counting the fly's operations, not measurements on a real board.

Chapter 12

Words to know

Connectome
The full wiring diagram of a brain: which neuron connects to which. The fruit fly's was completed in 2024.
Ommatidium
One of the tiny tubes of a compound eye; in our fly, one light sensor.
Ocelli
Three simple eyes on top of a fly's head that sense overall light and the horizon.
Halteres
A fly's tiny club-shaped hind wings, used as gyroscopes in flight.
Optic lobe
The visual part of the fly brain: lamina, medulla, lobula and lobula plate.
Lateral inhibition (edge boost)
Neighbouring cells dim each other, which makes edges stand out.
LC neurons
Visual feature-detector cells of the lobula that report things like small spots or looming objects. Our "eye cells" are modelled on them.
Mushroom body and Kenyon cells
The fly's learning centre. Each Kenyon cell reads a few random inputs, and only a few percent fire at once, so every picture gets its own sparse code.
Dopamine neurons, MBONs
Dopamine neurons carry reward and punishment into the mushroom body; its output neurons (MBONs) turn what was learned into "approach" or "avoid".
Gain adaptation
A cell turns its sensitivity up or down so that its usual signal has a standard size. Weak but telling signals then count as much as strong ones.
Fovea (sharp centre)
Sensors packed more tightly in the middle of the view.
Straight-line reader
A linear classifier: it adds up weighted evidence and answers by which side of a threshold the total lands.
Balanced accuracy
Accuracy where men and women count equally, so guessing always scores 50%.
Boosting
Training each new learner on the examples the earlier learners got wrong, then letting them all vote.
